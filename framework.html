01
Digital Word of Mouth

Turn your happiest patients into a 24/7 referral machine

Word of mouth is the best marketing that exists. A patient telling a friend "you have to go see Dr. [Name], she completely transformed my skin" is worth more than any ad you'll ever run. The problem is that, in its traditional form, word of mouth is invisible, uncontrollable and impossible to scale. Someone tells two friends at dinner, those friends don't tell anyone online, and three weeks later they've forgotten the clinic's name. Meanwhile a competitor across town has 340 Google reviews, a feed full of real patient results, and is chosen by strangers every day: people who've never met anyone who goes there, but trust what they see. That competitor isn't doing better marketing. They've just made word of mouth visible.

Part A · Your social media is either working for you or against you

When a patient hears about your clinic from a friend, the first thing they do is look you up on Instagram or Facebook. What they find in the next 30 seconds decides whether they book or keep scrolling. Generic stock photos, a post about "World Health Day" and a last update four months ago give them no reason to believe you're the right choice. Not because you're not good at what you do, but because the page gave them nothing to believe.

Your social media isn't a branding exercise. It's a proof portfolio, and it has to answer one question for a stranger: "Do people like me get results here?"

  • Patient results with the story attached. Not just a before/after image: what the concern was, what was done, how the patient feels now. This is the digital version of a friend telling a friend.
  • Treatment journey posts. A short video of the patient arriving, the process (nothing clinical or graphic) and leaving. It normalizes the experience for nervous people.
  • Behind-the-scenes content. The practitioner explaining a treatment in plain language, the clinic environment, the team. People trust you with their face and body; familiarity reduces fear.
  • Review screenshots and patient messages. When a patient sends "I'm obsessed with my results," that's a post. Blur the name if needed, keep the words.
Compliance: always get written consent before posting a patient's results. In markets like the US, UK and Canada, healthcare advertising has specific rules on before/after imagery, so check each platform's guidelines for your market before posting clinical results.
The goal of your social media isn't followers. It's trust. A page with 800 followers and 60 posts of real patient results will convert better than a page with 20,000 followers and generic content.

Part B · Google Maps is your most powerful free asset

Someone who types "aesthetic clinic near me" or "best med spa in [city]" is the most valuable prospect you'll ever meet. They aren't browsing and they aren't being interrupted by an ad. They're actively looking, right now, with intent to book. Clinics at the top of that search with 200+ reviews and a 4.8 rating capture that patient almost automatically. Clinics below them with 23 reviews and a 4.1 get skipped, even if they're genuinely better.

Your Google Business Profile isn't a formality. It's a patient acquisition channel that compounds: every review you collect today is still working for you five years from now.

  • Claim and fully complete the profile. Every field you fill in improves your ranking.
  • Add real photos of the clinic interior, the team and the equipment. Profiles with real photos get more clicks than those without.
  • Post updates regularly. Google treats this as a signal of an active, legitimate business.
  • Respond to every review, good and bad. Your reply to a negative review is read by every future patient. Handle it professionally and it becomes a trust signal instead of a liability.

Part C · The review-generation system

The number one reason clinics don't have enough reviews is simple: they never ask. Happy patients assume you don't need their review; they had a great time, told a friend and moved on. Unhappy patients always find the form. Asking can't be something that happens when the receptionist remembers. It has to be a system.

Send a WhatsApp or SMS within 24–48 hours of every appointment. Sent consistently, this one message will build your review base faster than anything else:

"Hi [Name], it was great seeing you today. We hope you're already loving your results. If you have 60 seconds, we'd be grateful if you could leave us a quick Google review. Honest feedback helps other patients find us and helps us keep improving. [Google Review Link]"

The 5% loyalty discount. To accelerate rebooking, send a thank-you message after the appointment with a 5% discount code for their next visit. In a separate follow-up, send the review request. The discount is a genuine thank-you for coming in, and it drives a rebooking at the same time.

Keep the two messages separate. Never offer the discount in exchange for a review. In most markets that violates Google's review policies. The discount is for coming in. The review request is because you value their feedback.
Before investing heavily in paid advertising, build a solid base of Google reviews with a strong average rating. Paid traffic lands on this profile, and if the profile doesn't convert, the ad spend is wasted.

02
The Value-Based Offer

The clinic that competes on price always loses. The clinic that competes on value wins.

Why most clinic offers fail

When most owners hear the word "offer," they think discount. So they run "20% off Botox this weekend" and two things happen. First, they attract patients who are shopping on price, and those patients leave the moment a competitor runs 25% off. Second, they train their existing patients to wait for a deal before booking. Discounts don't build a clinic. They erode one.

The offer isn't the price. The offer is the complete thing you're proposing: the outcome, the experience, the certainty and the ease. A great offer makes the price feel secondary because the value is so obvious that saying no feels like a mistake. The core idea, drawn from Alex Hormozi's $100M Offers: stop selling services, start selling outcomes.

The four components of a value-based offer

1 · Dream outcome

What does the patient actually want? Not the treatment name but the result they're picturing. They don't want "a Botox session." They want to look refreshed for their daughter's wedding in three weeks, to feel confident in photos again, to stop looking tired when they're not tired. Speak to that outcome in their language, not clinical language, and the service stops feeling like a procedure and starts feeling like the answer to something they've wanted for a long time.

2 · Perceived likelihood of success

Why will this work for them? There's a quiet voice saying "what if it doesn't work for me?" or "what if I look unnatural?" Your reviews, before/after results, credentials, consultation process and practitioner on camera do the heavy lifting here. The higher the perceived likelihood, the higher the value of the offer, without changing the price.

3 · Time delay

How quickly will they see results? Speed is value. "Visible improvement in one 45-minute session" is worth more than "results develop over 6 to 8 weeks," even when both are true. Name the timeframe. Specificity builds trust; vagueness creates doubt.

4 · Effort and sacrifice

What do they not have to do, endure or give up? No surgery. No downtime. No needles if they'd rather avoid them. Back to work the same day. Every fear or inconvenience you explicitly remove makes the offer stronger.

The offer formula

"[Dream outcome] in [specific timeframe], without [the fear or inconvenience they want to avoid], designed for [who they are]."
Med spa new-patient offer: "Look visibly refreshed for your next big occasion in one 45-minute session. No downtime, results that look natural not overdone, and a same-day consultation so you know exactly what to expect before we begin."

Dental clinic: "A complete smile assessment and same-day treatment plan in under an hour. No waiting weeks for results, no confusing jargon, just a clear picture of what's possible and what it costs, so you can make a confident decision."

There's no price drop and no discount in either. There is an outcome, a timeframe, removed friction and specificity. That is a value-based offer.

Build your own in four questions

  1. What is the one outcome my ideal patient is picturing when they think about coming in?
  2. What evidence do I have that it will work for them?
  3. What is the fastest honest result I can name?
  4. What are the top two or three things they fear or find inconvenient about this type of treatment?

Your offer is the answers to those four questions, written in one or two clear sentences.

Dream-outcome messaging, in the patient's language

03
Targeted Paid Traffic

The right person, seeing the right message, at the right moment

Why clinics who "tried ads" didn't fail at ads

Almost every owner who says "we tried Facebook ads and they didn't work" ran them before Steps 1 and 2 were in place. The Google profile had 11 reviews. The offer was "book a consultation." The landing page was the homepage. The ads brought traffic, and the traffic had nowhere to go.

Once your reputation is visible and your offer is compelling, paid traffic becomes a multiplier. You're no longer hoping strangers will trust a profile with no reviews and a generic offer. You're sending warm-intent audiences to a clinic that looks credible, with an offer that speaks directly to what they want.

Meta Ads vs. Google Ads: which one, and when

The two platforms solve different problems. Using the wrong one for the wrong objective wastes budget.

Meta (Facebook & Instagram) · interruption

The patient wasn't looking for you; they were scrolling, and your ad made them stop. This works exceptionally well for aesthetic treatments patients didn't know they wanted until they saw someone else's results: body contouring, skin treatments, anti-aging, non-surgical options. The ad creates the awareness and the desire. Meta is your top-of-funnel engine: it introduces your clinic to people who match your ideal patient by location, demographic and behavior before they've ever searched for you.

Google · intent

The patient is already looking. They typed "Botox clinic near me" or "laser hair removal [city]" right now, with the intent to find somewhere and book. Google captures demand that already exists. It's your bottom-of-funnel closer: the patient is already sold on the treatment, and your job is to be the clinic they find and choose.

Which to start with? If your Google profile and reviews are strong (Step 1 done), start with Google Search: lower volume, higher intent, higher close rate. If you're in a competitive market where Google clicks are expensive, or you want to build volume faster, add Meta, which creates demand and fills the top of your funnel with people who convert over days or weeks. Most established clinics eventually run both. Start with the one that matches your bottleneck: awareness or intent.

What actually works in clinic ads

  • On Meta: real faces outperform stock photos every time. Your practitioner on camera for 15–30 seconds, explaining a treatment in plain language and speaking directly to a concern, beats a designed graphic with a discount banner. Patients are trusting you with their face; they want to see yours first.
  • On Google: tight keyword control matters more than budget. Bidding on broad terms like "clinic" or "beauty treatments" wastes money on people who aren't your patient. Bid on treatment-specific, location-specific terms, and use negative keywords aggressively to stop paying for irrelevant searches.
  • On budget: there's no universal minimum that works in every market. A competitive city like London or New York needs more than a smaller one to get the same volume. The honest approach: start with what you can afford to lose while testing, measure cost per lead and cost per booked patient, and scale what's working.

04
The Conversion System

Traffic is only as good as what happens when it lands

The most expensive mistake in digital marketing

Sending paid traffic to your homepage. The homepage is built for existing patients and people already sold on your clinic: a menu with eight options, a photo carousel, a paragraph about your history, a newsletter signup and a "contact us" button at the bottom. A cold prospect lands on it, gets overwhelmed by the options, and leaves. The money spent on the ad is gone. Every paid campaign needs a dedicated landing page: one offer, one audience, one action.

What a clinic landing page that converts looks like

Above the fold
  • A headline that mirrors the ad. If the ad said "look refreshed in one session, no downtime," the headline says exactly that, not "Welcome to [Clinic Name]." The patient should feel an instant "yes, this is what I was looking for."
  • One visible call to action: book a consultation, claim your offer, get your assessment. One button, not three options.
  • A real image or short video of your clinic, team or a patient result (with consent). Never stock photography.
The body of the page
  • What the offer includes, in plain language: the experience, and what happens after they click. Specificity removes doubt.
  • Social proof: at least three real patient reviews, ideally with photos and names (with consent). This is where your Step 1 investment pays off.
  • Practitioner credibility in one short paragraph: enough to answer "why should I trust this person with my face."
  • A simple FAQ for the top objections: does it hurt, how long does it last, what if I don't like it, how much does it cost?

Lead form vs. direct booking

If your front desk or a VA can respond to a new lead within 5–10 minutes during business hours, use a lead form with two or three qualifying questions. It keeps friction low and gives you information before the call. If you can't guarantee a fast response, use a direct booking link so the patient books themselves into the calendar. A booking that needs no follow-up is always better than a lead that goes cold.

Qualifying questions that work (three maximum, since every extra field lowers completion):
• What treatment are you most interested in? (filters by service)
• What's your biggest concern about getting started? (surfaces objections early)
• How soon are you looking to come in? (filters urgency and intent)

05
Speed-to-Lead & Follow-Up

The clinic that responds first wins. Not the best clinic. The fastest one.

The number every clinic should know

Studies on lead response time consistently show that the odds of successfully reaching a lead fall sharply after the first five minutes. A lead who filled in your form at 2:03pm and got a call at 2:06pm is in a completely different conversation than one who waited until 5pm. By then they've gotten nervous, looked up another clinic and been called back by that one first. You lost them not because you're worse, but because you were slower.

This is the single biggest leverage point in patient acquisition once your ads are working, and it's almost invisible to clinic owners because the patient never tells you why they didn't book.

What fast follow-up looks like

The moment a lead comes in from a form, a DM or a Google inquiry, they receive an automated message within minutes. Not a generic "thanks for your inquiry," but something that confirms receipt, sets an expectation and asks a question that gets them to engage, which makes them far more likely to pick up when you call:

"Hi [Name], thanks for reaching out to [Clinic Name]. We've received your details and one of our team will call you within the next few minutes to answer any questions and get you booked in. Is now a good time to talk?"

The follow-up sequence for leads who don't pick up

Not every lead answers the first call. That doesn't mean they're not interested. They were busy, or driving, or with a patient of their own. A system that treats one unanswered call as a dead lead is burning money already spent generating the lead.

06
Retention & Lifetime Value

Your most profitable patient is the one already in your database

The number most clinic owners have never calculated

What is one patient worth to your clinic over their lifetime? If a patient comes in for Botox every four months, spends $400 per visit and continues for three years, that's $3,600 from one patient. If they refer two friends who do the same, that one patient generated over $10,000 in revenue.

Knowing this number changes how you think about marketing. It tells you exactly how much you can afford to spend acquiring a new patient and still be profitable, and it shows that keeping a patient is dramatically cheaper than finding a new one. Most clinics spend 100% of their marketing effort on new patients and almost nothing on the ones they already have.

The post-visit follow-up sequence

The 48 hours after an appointment are the highest-value window for building loyalty. The patient is experiencing results, thinking about you and deciding whether to come back.

24 hours afterA personal check-in: "Hi [Name], just checking in. How are you feeling after yesterday's session? Any questions or anything we can help with?" It shows care, opens a conversation, and gives them a moment to share a positive experience.
Review requestOnce they respond positively: "So glad to hear that. If you have a moment, we'd really appreciate a quick Google review. It helps other patients find us."
Rebooking promptAt the right interval for their treatment, before they forget: "Hi [Name], it's been [X weeks] since your last session. Based on your treatment, now is a great time to book your follow-up to maintain your results. Want me to check what's available?"

The reactivation campaign

Every clinic has a database of patients who came in once, had a good experience and disappeared, not because they were unhappy, but because life happened and nobody followed up. That database is a revenue source sitting dormant. A simple message to patients you haven't seen in 3–6 months:

"Hi [Name], it's been a while since we've seen you and we've been thinking about you. We have some availability coming up and wanted to reach out personally before opening it publicly. Is there anything we can help you with, or shall I check what's available for you?"

Personal, not promotional. No discount, just genuine outreach. You'll be surprised how many patients book from a message that simply acknowledges they've been missed.

The referral system

Happy patients refer, but most referrals happen by accident: a friend asks, the patient mentions you. A referral system makes it intentional. After a patient expresses satisfaction (in a message, in person or through a review), ask directly:

"We're so glad you're happy with your results. If you have any friends or family who've been thinking about [treatment], we'd love to take care of them too. If you refer someone, we'll make sure to look after you both."

An optional incentive, such as a treatment credit, a small gift or a discount on their next visit, acknowledges the introduction without making the relationship transactional.

The lifetime value formula

(Average spend per visit) × (visits per year) × (average years as a patient) + (referrals generated × their lifetime value)

You don't need a precise number. You need an estimate close enough to make smarter decisions about acquisition spend. Once you know it, the question "how much should I spend on ads?" answers itself.
